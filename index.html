The shift: from writing to guiding

  • Transition: "Let's start with the mindset."

From writer to editor

Before
  • You write every string from scratch
  • Quality depends on who wrote it
  • Guidelines live in a separate doc
After
  • You set the context and constraints
  • AI drafts, reviews, or offers options
  • Guidelines are built into the tools your team uses
  • You make the final call
  • This isn't about replacing writing skills. Editing well requires knowing what good copy is.
  • "Guidelines become tools" is the key idea for the whole session.

What LLMs are good and bad at in UX writing

  • Transition: "To guide AI well, you need to know its strengths and limits."

What LLMs do well

Following clear constraints

Voice guide, terminology, character limits, product facts

Keeping terms and format consistent

Select vs Choose, sentence case, date formats

Finding patterns across lots of copy

Inconsistent tone, structure, or formatting

Changing tone or complexity

Shorter, clearer, warmer, more formal, easier to translate

Critiquing and comparing options

Many options fast, plus feedback on your work

  • Overview only. Next slides go deeper on each.
  • The common thread: they're great when you provide the source of truth.

Following constraints and keeping copy consistent

UseAvoid
SelectChoose, Pick
Sign inLog in, Login
Save changesSave Changes
DeleteRemove (when data is gone)

Example terminology list you can paste into instructions

  • LLMs follow a voice guide, glossary, or character limit when you provide it
  • Many default to Title Case. You can teach sentence case with a rule and examples
  • One tool can't catch everything. Focus on your key inconsistencies
  • Build specialized tools for UI elements your team writes again and again: error messages, empty states, notifications
  • Title case is the classic default. Show how a single rule plus two examples fixes it.
  • Don't try to build one mega-prompt for all copy. Specialized tools work better.

Finding patterns and changing tone

Find patterns

Paste 50 strings from one flow and ask:

List inconsistent terms, capitalization, tone, and sentence structure. Group them by type and show where each one appears.

Useful before a release or a design system audit.

Change tone or complexity
Original
Your request could not be processed due to an unexpected error.
Shorter and clearer
We couldn't save your changes. Try again.
Easier to translate
Your changes were not saved. Try again.
  • Pattern finding is where LLMs beat humans: they don't get tired at string 200.
  • Point out that the meaning stayed the same across versions. That's what you check.

What LLMs don't do well

Writing from scratch

Especially without context. The copy comes out generic and often doesn't fit your product.

Product context

Copy can sound plausible but describe something the product doesn't do.

Dependencies between products

It won't know another feature already has the same name.

Knowing what's clear to real users

Copy that seems simple to the model can still confuse people in the actual flow.

  • All four have one root cause: the model doesn't know what you know.

Always bring a rough first draft

No context
Prompt
Write an error message for a failed upload.
Output
Oops! Something went wrong. Please try again later.
Draft and context
Prompt
Draft: "upload failed, file too big". Limit is 25 MB. Inline, under the drop zone. Improve it.
Output
report.pdf is larger than 25 MB. Upload a smaller file or compress it first.
  • Even a bad draft carries the facts: what happened, the limit, where it appears.
  • "Try again later" is wrong here: trying again won't help. The model can't know that.

LLMs don't know your product or the products around it

Plausible but wrong
Don't worry, your changes are saved automatically.

This is wrong if the product doesn't autosave.

Hidden conflict
Introducing Workflows: automate your daily tasks.

Another product in your suite already has a feature called Workflows.

What helps: give product facts and system behavior, keep a glossary across products, and check facts with your PM and engineers.
  • The bigger the product, the more these matter.
  • A shared glossary is the cheapest fix for naming conflicts.

Copy that seems clear to the model can still confuse your users

LLMs can critique copy and generate options, but they can't replace usability testing. Test copy with real users in the actual flow.

  • Psychology angle: comprehension depends on context, expectations, and stress level, none of which the model sees.

Generative vs evaluative tools

Tools that generate and tools that evaluate

Generative

Creates copy

Drafts, variants, tone shifts, shorter versions, options to address feedback.

Evaluative

Checks copy

Compares copy against your rules: terms, case, length, tone, accessibility.

Tool typeGenerateEvaluateBest for
Figma plugins and toolsSometimesYesIn-place evaluation: copy in the context of the design and layout
LLMs, such as Claude skillsYesYesGenerating and evaluating anywhere text lives: docs, strings, screenshots

Structuring instructions

  • Transition: "Tools are only as good as the instructions behind them."

Why structure your instructions

For the model

It follows rules in sections and lists more reliably than rules in one long paragraph.

For your team

Anyone can read, review, and improve the same instructions.

For your tools

Markdown is the shared format for prompts, Claude skills, custom GPTs, and project rules.

  • Markdown isn't code. If you can write a bulleted list, you can write markdown.

Markdown basics

You typeYou getUse it in prompts for
# HeadingMain headingName of the tool or task
## SectionSubheadingSections: Context, Rules, Examples, Output
- itemBulleted listRules and constraints
1. stepNumbered listSteps the model must follow in order
**bold**BoldRules that must never be broken
| a | b |TableTerminology lists and output format
  • Use bold sparingly. If everything is important, nothing is.

What to include in a copy instruction

1

Task

What to do and for which UI element

2

Context

Users, product facts, where the copy appears

3

Rules

Voice, terminology, case, character limits

4

Examples

Good and bad, with a reason for each

5

Output

Format you want back: options, table, rationale

  • Most bad outputs trace back to a missing block, usually Context or Examples.

Example: an error message review skill

---
name: error-message-review
description: Review and rewrite error messages. Use when someone shares an error, toast, or inline validation.
---

# Error message reviewer

## Task
Review the error message. Then suggest up to 3 rewrites.

## Context
Ask for these if they're missing:
- Where it appears (toast, inline, modal)
- What caused the error and what the user can do next

## Rules
- Say what happened and what to do next.
- Use sentence case. Use "select", not "choose". Use "sign in", not "log in".
- **Never blame the user.** Avoid "invalid", "illegal", and "failed to".
- Toasts: 90 characters max.

## Examples
Bad: "Invalid input. Error 422."  Good: "Enter a date in the future, like 12/31/2026."

## Output
A table with: Issue | Why it matters | Suggested fix
  • Point to each of the five blocks in the file.
  • "Ask for these if missing" stops the model from guessing product facts.

Best practices for writing instructions

  • Be specific. Write "60 characters max", not "keep it short".
  • Explain why. The model applies a rule better when it knows the reason.
  • Show good and bad examples. Examples often work better than more rules.
  • Say what to do, not only what to avoid.
  • Give each tool one job. Use separate tools for errors, empty states, and notifications.
  • Ask for missing context. Tell the model to ask instead of guessing.
  • Keep it short. Remove rules it already follows.
  • Version and share it. Maintain it like any other design system asset.
  • Highlight "one tool, one job". It ties back to building specialized tools.

Evals: how to test

  • Transition: "How do you know your instructions actually work?"

What's an eval?

A repeatable test that checks whether your prompt or tool does what you expect across many examples.

1 · Collect

10–20 real test cases, including edge cases: long names, plurals, legal copy, tricky terms

→
2 · Define

Clear pass or fail criteria, written as a checklist

→
3 · Run and score

Score by hand, or use an LLM as a judge and spot-check it

→
4 · Compare

Change one thing, rerun, compare results

  • Change one thing at a time, or you won't know what helped.

Example: an eval sheet

Test caseSentence caseApproved termsFits limitNext stepNo blameResult
File too large✓✓✓✓✓Pass
Session expired✓✗ "Log in"✓✓✓Fail
Wrong password✗✓✓✓✗ "Invalid"Fail
No internet✓✓✗ 112 chars✓✓Fail
For each failure, add a rule, an example, or more context. Then rerun the whole set.
  • A spreadsheet is enough. No special tools needed to start.
  • "Log in" slipped through: the glossary rule needs an example.

Eval tips

  • Keep a reference set: strings you know are right, to compare against
  • Rerun after every change to instructions, and when the model updates
  • Log failures and turn each one into a rule or example

AI can check rules

Case, terms, length, words to avoid

People judge meaning

Tone, empathy, clarity, fit for the situation

  • LLM-as-judge is useful but needs spot-checking. It shares the same blind spots.

Common issues when generating copy with LLMs

  • Transition: "Here's what to look for when you review."

Common issues and what helps

IssueWhat helps
Title Case by defaultA sentence case rule with examples
Generic filler: "Oops!", "seamlessly", "unlock the power of"A list of words to avoid, plus examples in your voice
Too long: LLMs count characters poorlyCheck length with a tool, ask for several lengths
Invented features or system behaviorProduct facts, and ask it to flag assumptions
Terms change between runsA glossary in the instructions
Meaning lost when shorteningAsk it to list what it removed
Over-friendly: exclamation marks and emoji in errorsTone rules for each context
Accessibility gaps: "Click here", "see the panel on the right"Accessibility rules, plus human review
  • Don't read the whole table. Pick 3: title case, invented features, accessibility.
  • Accessibility: descriptive link text, no directional or color-only cues.

AI-powered content design workflows

  • Transition: "Let's put it together for your team."

Workflow for content designers

Build

Turn guidelines into skills and evaluation tools

→
Draft

You and designers draft with the tools

→
Review

You review the copy that matters most

→
Improve

Run evals and update instructions

You build and maintain the tools, edit copy, and own its quality. Your guidelines get applied even when you're not reviewing.
  • This is how you become invaluable: you scale your expertise.

Workflow for teams with a consulting content designer

Designers
  • Use shared tools for everyday copy: labels, tooltips, helper text
  • Run the evaluation tool before asking for review
  • Bring a draft and context to every review
Content designer
  • Builds and maintains the shared tools and rules
  • Reviews high-risk copy: errors, onboarding, pricing, legal, AI disclosures
  • Turns recurring review comments into new rules
  • The feedback loop is key: the same comment twice means a missing rule.

Workflow for teams without content designers

  • Create one page with your voice, key terms, and formatting rules
  • Build one review tool for the UI element you write most
  • Peer review with a shared checklist
  • Borrow from your design system docs and public style guides
  • Test copy with real users in usability sessions
  • Bring in a content designer for an audit when you can
Without a content designer, evals and user testing matter even more.
  • Don't wait for a perfect style guide. One page is enough to start.

Review checklist for any team

  • Clear: says what happened and what to do next
  • Accurate: matches what the product actually does
  • Consistent: approved terms, sentence case, formats
  • Concise: fits the space, no filler
  • Empathetic: right tone for the situation, no blame
  • Accessible: descriptive links, no direction or color-only cues, works with screen readers
  • Translatable: no idioms, no strings built from fragments
Use the same checklist for human review, AI evaluation tools, and evals.
  • One checklist, three uses. That's what makes quality independent of who wrote it.

Principles and next steps

  • Transition: "Let's close with how to do this responsibly."

Principles for using AI responsibly

You make the call

AI suggests options. You decide and you're responsible for the result.

Use approved tools

Don't paste confidential designs or customer data into public tools.

Bring context

Share a draft, product facts, and constraints.

Verify facts

Check system behavior with your PM and engineers.

Test with people

Only usability testing shows what users actually understand.

Check accessibility

Review every AI suggestion for accessibility before you use it.

  • These build credibility with your team: you're the one using AI carefully, not blindly.
